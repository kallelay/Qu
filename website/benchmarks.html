Measured, including the losses

Qu against MATLAB R2025b and NumPy, on the same machine, in the same session — on speed and, separately, on whether the answers are right. Qu loses four of five speed kernels to MATLAB. It ties every accuracy test to the last digit that matters. Both of those are the result.

2026-09-11 · Ryzen 9700X, 16 logical cores · Qu release build, MATLAB R2025b, CPython + NumPy

How these were measured

Data allocated outside the clock, one warm-up discarded, best of three, languages interleaved. This matters more than it sounds. An earlier version of this suite timed one cold run per language and had Qu winning four of five kernels. Measured fairly, Qu won none of those four. MATLAB's JIT needs one call to warm up and then wins; a single cold trial cannot see that. Three published claims on this site did not survive the correction, and were withdrawn rather than quietly dropped.

Against NumPy, Qu is 48% faster overall and wins three kernels of five. Against MATLAB, Qu wins only matrix multiplication and is 46% behind overall. Seconds, best of three.

Where the MATLAB gap actually lives

element_wise_ops is 65.5% of the total runtime and carries a 1.79× gap. Everything else is rounding error by comparison — loop_performance, for instance, is 0.8% of total, so even a large improvement there cannot move the headline.

Separately measured, in three interleaved pairs: Qu's elementwise kernel alone runs about 8% faster than MATLAB's entire element_wise_ops computation, and interpreter dispatch accounts for 48% of Qu's wall time — 48%, 48%, 48% across the three pairs. So the gap is not numerics. It is the cost of deciding what to call before calling it.

The 1,048,576-point FFT

Scaling one kernel up asks a question the small sizes cannot: does the gap grow, hold, or close? It does both, depending on the length.

Transform lengthQu / MATLABReading

On power-of-two and 3-smooth lengths the ratio is flat at about 2.7× across four orders of magnitude, which is the signature of a constant per-call overhead — the same dispatch cost as above, not an algorithmic one. As the factorisation gets rougher the gap closes, and at a genuine prime length it reverses: both languages pay a large penalty on prime-length transforms, and MATLAB's is far worse — roughly 26× its own power-of-two time against Qu's 5×, leaving Qu about twice as fast there.

A retraction, kept rather than quietly edited out. An earlier version of this page reported that the gap grew to 3.14× at 1,000,000 points, citing two interleaved passes. It does not reproduce. Re-measured against live MATLAB at the same length, best of three, it is 1.32×. Qu's own timings for that single length moved about 40% from pass to pass on this shared machine — enough to account for the original figure entirely. The number was ours, the error was ours, and the correction is ours to publish.

Two things were checked before blaming the FFT implementation. Qu already caches its plan — FFT_PLANNER is a reused thread-local, so there is no per-call planning cost to remove. And Qu's own wrapper overhead (the complex-number copy in and out) measured 0.96×–1.22× of raw library time at every size, which is not where the difference lives.

Accuracy

Speed comparisons are common; accuracy comparisons are rarer, and for anyone who publishes numbers they are the ones that matter. Each test below has a closed-form ground truth — an answer known exactly in advance, independent of all three implementations.

FFT against an analytic spectrum

Three cosines placed on exact bin centres, so the true transform is known in closed form. All three languages agree with it — and with each other — to the identical absolute error:

QuantityQuMATLABNumPy
max error, signal bins9.20e-69.20e-69.20e-6
noise floor, other bins1.55e-71.55e-71.55e-7

Identical, not merely close: the same transform computed to the same precision.

Filter design against closed-form poles and zeros

Qu's freqz against the analytic frequency response, over a 513-point sweep: 2×10-15 maximum error at order 6, still 8×10-15 at order 20. No conditioning trouble at these orders.

MATLAB is absent from this comparison, and the reason is worth stating plainly: butter and freqz require the Signal Processing Toolbox, which is not licensed on this machine. Hand-rolling a bilinear transform to stand in for MATLAB's answer would have meant comparing Qu against our own code and labelling it MATLAB. The column is marked unavailable instead. Qu ships filter design in the base language.

Equivalent-circuit fitting: recovering known parameters

A Randles circuit with known component values, synthesised into an impedance spectrum, then fitted back by Nelder–Mead in all three languages. Same algorithm class everywhere, deliberately: letting each language reach for its own best optimiser would measure the optimisers rather than the languages.

After the fit, all three agree on the residual (6.11e-17), on the recovered values, and — more tellingly — on the iteration count. Identical trajectories, not merely identical answers.

A model can fit beautifully and still be wrong

The same fit, run twice. Everything is held constant except the measured frequency range.

Frequency sweepResidualRct errorIterations
1–10 kHz — stops short of the ~159 kHz RC knee2.01e-1042.4%126
Full range — covers the knee6.11e-172.3e-05%159

The bad sweep fits the data it was given almost perfectly and gets a component value wrong by 42%. Nothing in the residual warns you. The information needed to determine Rct lives near the RC knee, and a sweep that never reaches it cannot recover the parameter no matter how well the curve matches.

This is what parameter-significance testing exists to detect, and it is why a fitting tool should report whether a parameter is identifiable rather than only how small the residual got.

Two things that nearly became false claims

Both were caught by the ground truth, and both are worth recording because neither would have looked like a mistake.

A bug in our own MATLAB port

The MATLAB implementation of the circuit fit had a redundant +1 in an index helper, on top of callers that were already passing 1-based indices. Every update wrote to a phantom cell, so the optimiser sat at its starting guess for 2000 iterations and never moved.

That is indistinguishable from “MATLAB is worse at this”, and it would have been published as a cross-language accuracy gap — except Qu and NumPy had already converged to the exact known answer, so a MATLAB result that did not could only be our own error. A timing-only benchmark has nothing to contradict it with.

A library default that silently moved the goalposts

SciPy's sosfreqz, given an integer worN, excludes the Nyquist endpoint. Qu's convention includes it. Every comparison point would have been misaligned by one bin — surfacing not as an obvious crash but as a small, plausible accuracy disagreement. Caught by checking the convention instead of assuming it, and the axes are now passed explicitly.

What this does not tell you

  • One machine, one session. Ratios on other hardware will differ.
  • Five kernels and three accuracy tests are not a workload. They were chosen to be measurable and checkable, not representative.
  • MATLAB's Signal Processing and Optimization Toolboxes are not licensed here, so two comparisons are two-language rather than three.
  • Qu is faster than NumPy overall and slower than MATLAB overall. Neither sentence is the whole picture, and the per-kernel table is the honest version of both.
